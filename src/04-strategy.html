How to actually play this game

Every number on this page was computed by exhaustive enumeration of the 36-card deck, not estimated. The preflop hand ranking uses Monte Carlo simulation.

1. The one thing to understand: equities run close

Take out the 2s through 5s and every hand gets better. But your opponent's hand gets better too, and it gets better by roughly the same amount. The result is that the gap between a great hand and a mediocre hand shrinks.

What this means at the table: pushing a small preflop edge all-in is worth much less than in holdem. AA versus KK pays you 74.6% instead of 81%. QQ is an actual underdog to AK. Because nothing dominates, post-flop skill and position matter more, and pre-flop all-in gambling matters less.

2. The rule of 3 and 6 (not 2 and 4)

In holdem after the flop there are 47 cards you have not seen, so each out is worth about 1/47 = 2.1% per card. Multiply outs by 2 for one card, by 4 for two cards.

In short deck there are only 31 unseen cards after the flop (36 minus your 2 minus the 3 on the board). Each out is worth 1/31 = 3.2% per card. So:

Rule of 3

One card to come (turn to river): outs × 3. With 30 unseen it is actually 3.3% per out, so this slightly under-counts — which is the safe direction.

Rule of 6

Two cards to come (flop to river): outs × 6. Accurate up to about 6 outs, then it starts to over-count. See the table.

Where the rule of 6 breaks: it is good to about 6 outs and then drifts high, the same way the rule of 4 does in holdem but worse. At 8 outs it says 48% and the truth is 45.6%. At 12 outs it says 72% and the truth is 63.2%. For big draws, use the rule of 3 twice and subtract a little, or just remember: a big combo draw is around 60-65%, never 70%+.

3. Flush draws are weak. Straight draws are strong.

This is the single biggest adjustment coming from holdem, and it is pure counting.

There are only nine cards in each suit. If you hold two of a suit and two more are on the flop, that is four of the nine accounted for — five left, not nine. Meanwhile an open-ended straight draw still has its full eight outs, because straights depend on ranks and no rank was removed, only whole low ranks.

Practical version: stop paying big prices for flush draws. Start paying them for open-enders. A flush draw in short deck is worth roughly what a gutshot-plus is worth in holdem. And when you DO make a flush, it is close to unbeatable — it beats a full house.

5. The 81 starting hands, ranked

Short deck has 630 two-card combinations, but before a board exists the suits are interchangeable, so there are exactly 81 strategically different starting hands (9 pairs, 36 suited, 36 offsuit). Here they are ranked by equity against a random hand.

The top five pairs occupy the top five slots outright. Then it falls off a cliff: 66 is 78th out of 81 hands. In holdem, 22 keeps value because it can flop a set that nobody sees coming. In short deck, 66 makes the worst set, the worst straight, is dominated by every other pair, and there are only three ranks below the 9 to hide behind. Small pairs are close to unplayable here and that surprises almost everyone.

One honest caveat about this list. "Equity against a random hand" measures raw strength in an all-in, not how playable a hand is. It systematically flatters big offsuit cards and punishes connectors: 76s shows up 79th, but a hand that makes straights and flushes plays far better after the flop than a hand like J6o that can only make one weak pair. 6. Core principles the solver plays by

Where these come from. The principles below follow directly from the maths on this page — they are not received poker wisdom, and every one of them can be checked against a number above. To see what the solver itself actually does, hand by hand and street by street, use the GTO Solver tab: it lets you walk the solved tree and read the real frequencies.

Play more hands, fold less

Every hand is closer to every other hand. The gap between the best and worst starting hand is far smaller than in holdem, so tight-passive is a losing style here.

Position is worth more

Because equities run close, the hand that acts last wins more of the marginal pots. The button posts the extra ante for exactly this reason.

Respect the flush

Flushes beat full houses and only 480 exist. If a lot of money goes in on a three-suited board, someone has it.

Top pair is a bluff-catcher

Not a value hand. Everyone holds big cards, so everyone pairs big boards. Stacking off with top pair is the classic short deck leak.

Sets are worth a stack

You flop them more often and they hold up more often, because the main thing that beats them - a flush - almost never comes.

Draws are betting hands

An open-ender is often the mathematical favourite against a made overpair. It should be raising, not calling.
