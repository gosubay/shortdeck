The rules of Short Deck

Also called 6+ Hold'em or Six Plus. Same shape as No Limit Hold'em, but four ranks are missing and two hand rankings change because of it.

1. The deck: 36 cards, not 52

Every 2, 3, 4 and 5 is removed. What is left is 6, 7, 8, 9, T, J, Q, K, A in all four suits — nine ranks, 36 cards.

Why this changes everything: you have removed 16 of the 20 lowest cards. Every hand that gets dealt is made of big cards, so everybody connects with the board far more often. Top pair is no longer a strong hand. Two pair is routine. The average winning hand at showdown goes up a full rank compared to holdem.

The Ace is still both high and low

The Ace plays as the card below the 6, so A-6-7-8-9 is a straight — the short deck "wheel". It is the lowest straight, exactly as A-2-3-4-5 is in holdem. That means the Ace is part of two straights (A6789 and TJQKA), same as always.

Why a flush beats a full house here

There are only nine cards of each suit instead of thirteen. Making five of them becomes hard: flushes fall from 5,108 combos to just 480. Full houses only fall from 3,744 to 1,728. So the flush flips from being roughly 1.4× more common than a boat to being 3.6× rarer — and the ranking follows the rarity.

Why a straight still beats trips

This is the one people argue about, and the maths settles it. Yes, straights get easier to make relative to holdem — but trips get harder faster. Straights drop from 10,200 to 6,120 combos (down 40%). Trips drop from 54,912 to 16,128 (down 71%). Trips remain 2.6× more common than straights, so the straight stays above it.

Some older home games rank trips above straights. This trainer does not, and that is the correct Triton-style ruleset. If trips beat straights, open-ended straight draws become close to worthless and the whole game distorts.

A quirk nobody re-ranks

Look at the bottom of the table: high card (122,400) is actually rarer than one pair (193,536) in short deck. By pure frequency logic, high card "should" beat a pair. No game does this — it would be unplayable — but it tells you something useful: an unpaired, undrawing hand in short deck is close to worthless. You will be paired or better most of the time just by accident.

3. The betting: antes, not blinds

Short deck is normally an ante game. Instead of two blinds, every player puts in one ante before the cards, and the button puts in an extra one.

Heads-up, as played in this trainer:

  • Both players ante 1A. ("A" = one ante, the unit of money here.)
  • The button posts an extra 1A button ante, so the button is in for 2A total and the opponent for 1A.
  • The starting pot is 3A.
  • Because the button has the biggest forced bet, the button acts last preflop as well as last on every later street. This is the opposite of heads-up holdem, where the button acts first before the flop.
Plain version: the button here works like the big blind and the dealer button combined. It costs more, but it has position on every single street. That is a real edge, and it is why the non-button has to fight so hard preflop.

After that it is standard: flop (3 cards), turn (1 card), river (1 card), no-limit betting on each, best five-card hand wins.

4. Stack rules used in this trainer

  • Everyone starts at 50A.
  • Fall below 10A and you are automatically topped back up to 50A.
  • Rise above 100A and the excess is skimmed off and banked — you keep the money, but you sit back down with 100A.

So live stacks always sit between 10A and 100A. This is deliberate: it means the solver only ever has to know how to play a known, bounded set of stack depths, which is what makes a solved bot possible on a laptop instead of a data centre.

5. Quick differences cheat sheet

Flush draws are weak

Only 5 outs, not 9. There are just nine cards per suit and you can see four of them. A flush draw is now worse than an open-ended straight draw.

Straight draws are strong

Still 8 outs, and every out is worth more because fewer cards remain unseen. Connectors go up in value; suited cards go down.

Top pair is not a made hand

It is a bluff-catcher. Everyone has big cards, so everyone pairs the big board cards.

Sets and straights are the money

You flop a set more often (fewer cards to dodge) and it stays good more often, since flushes are rare.

Aces run worse

AA versus KK is 74.6% here, not 81%. Everything runs closer together, so getting all-in preflop is less profitable than you think.

Rule of 3 and 6, not 2 and 4

With only 31 unseen cards after the flop, each out is worth about 3% per card instead of 2%. See the Strategy tab.
